The basics, in plain language

When you buy a stock, you rarely buy it from another investor at that exact moment. Usually you buy from a market maker: a firm that is always willing to sell at a slightly higher price (the ask) and buy at a slightly lower one (the bid). The gap between them is the spread, and it is how the market maker earns a living.

Some of the biggest market makers trade at speeds measured in microseconds. That is what people mean by high-frequency trading (HFT). It is a controversial topic, so the project starts with the fundamentals instead of taking a side.

Why the spread exists

Two risks make market making hard.

  • Adverse selection. Some people trading with you know more than you do. If a buyer knows the price is about to rise, selling to them loses money. The spread is partly compensation for that.
  • Inventory risk. Each trade changes what the market maker holds. A long position loses money if the price falls, so holding too much of anything is dangerous.

Our simulation focuses on the second risk. Our reading list covers the first.

What this project does

Two parts, built in the open so anyone can check the work.

  1. A simulation (finished). We implemented the classic market-making model from Avellaneda and Stoikov (2008) and compared it with a naive quoter.
  2. A study of real trades (started). We measure volatility, estimated spreads and buy/sell pressure using free public Binance data, and test whether they move together the way theory predicts. A first week of data is analyzed below, with mixed results.

What this page does not claim. The simulation results come from made-up price paths. They show how the model behaves, not that anyone could profit from it in a real market. The real-trade results are a first look at one week of one coin.

Results so far

We simulated one trading session 1,000 times for two market makers who face the same random prices and the same random buyers and sellers. The only difference is how they set quotes.

  • Symmetric quoter. Always quotes the same distance either side of the current price.
  • Inventory-aware quoter (Avellaneda-Stoikov). Moves both quotes down when it is holding too much stock, to attract buyers, and up when it is short.

1. The market has a daily rhythm

We measured how much the price jumps around within each minute (realized volatility, in basis points, where 100 bps is 1%). Volatility is mostly highest from 14:00 to 21:00 UTC, which covers the US trading day, and generally lower at other times.

2. Buying and price move together

For each minute we computed buy pressure: buy volume minus sell volume, divided by total volume, where a buy means the buyer was the one who started the trade. In the same minute, buy pressure and price change have a correlation of 0.43. That is strong, but it is partly built in: buyers who start trades push the price up as they trade.

3. Does buy pressure predict the next minute? Not clearly.

The more interesting question is whether this minute's buy pressure helps predict the next minute's price change. We sorted 10,079 minutes into five equal groups by buy pressure.

The two most buying-heavy groups are followed by small gains, but the pattern is not steady across groups. The correlation with the next minute is 0.01, and the statistical test gives 1.5, below the usual cutoff of about 2. With this week of data we cannot say buy pressure predicts the next minute. We also cannot rule it out, because a week is short and the effect, if real, is small.

4. Estimating the spread from trades did not work well

Without quote data, the standard shortcut is the Roll (1984) estimator, which infers the spread from how trade prices bounce. On this data the estimator had no valid answer in 73% of hours, because trade prices tended to keep moving in one direction instead of bouncing. In the 46 hours where it worked, hours with higher volatility had higher estimated spreads (rank correlation 0.63), which matches theory. But those hours are a selected subset, and the estimates probably mix in price impact from large orders. We do not treat them as measured spreads.

What we learned. Trade data alone is not enough to study spreads. The next step is data with bids and asks, such as order book snapshots, so we can measure the spread directly instead of guessing it.

Reading list

Three papers anchor the project. We link to where each can be read and summarize each in our own words.

Glosten and Milgrom (1985), Journal of Financial Economics

Shows why spreads exist even when market makers earn no extra profit: some traders are informed, so quotes must protect against them. Read it first. Publisher page (may need library access).

Avellaneda and Stoikov (2008), Quantitative Finance

Sets up the market maker's problem as balancing inventory risk against the chance of getting filled, and derives the quotes we simulate. Author's PDF.

Brogaard, Hendershott and Riordan (2014), Review of Financial Studies

An empirical look at what high-frequency traders actually do, based on NASDAQ data. Their findings on price discovery are the model for our real-data section. Free working-paper version.

Limits you should know about

  • The simulation ignores queue position, network delay, trading fees and minimum price increments. Real high-frequency firms compete on exactly those things.
  • Trade-only data cannot give true quoted spreads. We will use an estimate and say so.
  • Crypto spot markets are not the US stock market. Findings describe market structure, not NASDAQ.
  • This is a student project. Treat it as a learning resource, not as financial advice.
